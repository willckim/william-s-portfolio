This is scenario analysis, not a forecast. The presets and sliders adjust around the real macro forecast, they do not predict it. The spread comes from redrawing weather, prices, rates and the macro forecast's past misses 1,000 times: a spread of scenario outcomes, not a probability. Months 13 to 24 are a scenario extension, not a forecast. The macro forecast and its backtest cover 12 months. Beyond that, base bookings repeat the macro P50's 12-month seasonal shape with no further growth.

How the winner is picked: seasonal naive, a linear trend with seasonality, and damped Holt-Winters are each tested by forecasting from every recent period in turn and comparing with what came next. The lowest average error wins, with ties going to the simpler model. The band is the range of that model's own past misses, not a probability. This is the forecasting engine's Python, ported to JavaScript, and the site's checks confirm the two agree on the samples.

How it works

What is real and what is fictional

Real: the macro forecast of U.S. private nonresidential construction spending, its backtest, the O'Hare weather history, and the price and rate series, all public data pulled without API keys on 2026-10-04. Fictional: Prairie Ridge Builders (fictional). Its backlog, bookings, margins and cost shares are synthetic, written for this demo, and every one is labeled in the engine's config as sourced or as an assumption.

The macro forecast and its backtest

The target is PNRESCON, in millions of dollars per month, not seasonally adjusted. Six models were tested with a rolling-origin backtest: 71 monthly forecast origins from Sep 2020 to Jul 2026, each forecasting 1 to 12 months ahead. The rule for picking the published model was fixed before any backtest ran: lowest average MAPE over 1 to 12 months. It was run twice: lag 0 treats a month as known when it ends, lag 1 treats it as known a month later, which is about when Census publishes it. The published forecast uses lag 1.

A flagged weekday is a partly productive day, not a lost day. Each rule has a light and a severe tier, and a day takes the lowest factor among the rules it trips. Scenario assumptions

Tariffs, named by mechanism only. Each episode's size is the change in metal prices over its window, pushed through pass-through coefficients estimated from 2015-01 to 2026-08 monthly data (R squared 0.6493). Raised is 1 times the episode, reduced gives back half (an assumption), held is zero.

  • 2025 to 2026 episode (default). Jan 2025 to Aug 2026. Log change WPU1017 +37.7%, WPU102501 +40.7%, WPU102502 +23.0%. Through the pass-through coefficients that is 4.56% on the construction input PPI. From the last month before the first 2025 metals action to the latest month in the snapshot. It reflects current tariff levels, so it is the default. Over a 19-month window the price change also includes global price and demand moves, so treat it as the larger estimate. Federal Register actions in the window: Proclamations 10895 and 10896, aluminum and steel, Federal Register 2025-02-18, Proclamation 10947, aluminum and steel, Federal Register 2025-06-09, Proclamation 10962, copper, Federal Register 2025-08-05, Proclamations 11021, 11032 and 11045, aluminum, steel and copper, Federal Register 2026-04-09 to 2026-07-23.
  • 2018 episode. Feb 2018 to Aug 2018. Log change WPU1017 +15.0%, WPU102501 +6.3%. Through the pass-through coefficients that is 0.91% on the construction input PPI. Six months around the 2018 steel and aluminum tariffs. Copper is left out because it was not covered and fell over the window.

Rates. History cannot separate how rates affect construction from how the Fed responds to booms: every regression the engine ran says higher rates go with more construction growth later (+0.021 to +0.049 per 100 bp). So the scenario uses a labeled assumption: bookings fall 5% per +100 bp, after 6 months. The size comes from Bloesch and Weber, "Structural Changes in Investment and the Waning Power of Monetary Policy", Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Reports no. 1190, March 2026. The 5% is the peak response of nonresidential structures SPENDING to a 100 bp CUT. Applying it to bookings, and to a rise, assumes the effect is symmetric. That is an assumption. In that paper the spending response peaks about 10 quarters after the shock. Bookings lead spending, so the 6-month lag on bookings is an assumption. The estimate uses 1969 to 1996 data, and Chicago Fed Letter 482 (2023) reports a positive response of structures to higher rates, which it calls puzzling. The sign is not settled. The base path follows the Fed's September 2026 projections, from 3.75% in Sep 2026 to 4.1% at the end of 2026, 4.1% at the end of 2027, 3.9% at the end of 2028, 3.6% at the end of 2029.

Public funding. A delayed surface transportation bill holds back 60% of public bookings for 6 months. An assumption.

Fuel. Diesel moves to the 10th or 90th percentile of its historical 12-month change (-23.7% or +30.4% in log terms) over the first year. Escalation clauses here cover materials, not fuel.

The company. Materials are 40% of cost, escalation clauses pass 80% of a materials cost change to the owner, 50% of work is weather-exposed, and new bookings start 2 months after signing and run 12 months. Base bookings are trailing bookings scaled by the macro forecast's growth. The synthetic backlog:

Personal project · Post-Quantum Readiness Toolkit

Which finance records are already exposed?

Harvest now, decrypt later: an adversary can record encrypted traffic today and read it once a quantum computer can break RSA and elliptic-curve keys, so any record that must stay secret past that day is at risk now. Mosca's inequality turns that into arithmetic. If the years a record must stay secret (x) plus the years a migration takes (y) exceed the years until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (z), the record is exposed.

* marks an assumption, not a retention rule. Margin is how many years a record stays sensitive after the quantum computer arrives, or how many years it has to spare. Retention periods are a lower bound on how long data must stay secret, so these results understate exposure. Scenarios are planning windows, not predictions.

The full toolkit goes further than this calculator: it scans code for quantum-vulnerable encryption and produces a prioritized migration register, with evidence on every finding, plus a CycloneDX CBOM export.

Qiskit programs run on a local simulator, each compared against the classical method an analyst would use today. This is study, not production quantum work, and the losses stay in.

Grover search, step by step

16 states, one marked. The oracle flips the marked state's sign, then diffusion reflects every amplitude about the mean. Step through it and watch the marked amplitude grow. This is the real algorithm on a 16-number state vector, computed in your browser.

The optimum for 16 states is iterations, a chance of the marked state. The repo's grover_search.py marks state 7 and runs isqrt(16) = 4 iterations, one past the optimum, so its 1,000 shots sample from a chance instead. The repo does not save its measured counts, so there is no recorded hit rate to quote. Step to 4 here to see the distribution those shots come from.

Monte Carlo risk: the quadratic speedup

The probability a small business runs low on cash during the year, estimated two ways. Classical error falls like 1/√N, amplitude estimation error like 1/M, so halving the error costs 4x the samples but only 2x the queries.

To reach 0.5% error: 1,024 classical samples, 900 quantum oracle queries. The reference probability is 0.0522, from 200,000 classical simulations.

The honest caveat: the quantum side uses a one-qubit oracle calibrated to the same probability, because loading the full cash-flow model into a circuit is the hard, open part. On a laptop simulator, classical still finishes first in wall-clock time. The advantage shown is in query counts.
